GUIDE 02 · RISK AWARENESS

Risk concepts: know the limits of a calculation

Risk is not a single number. It includes uncertainty about what may happen, how much may be lost, whether an order can execute as expected, and whether a decision fits a person's circumstances.

Risk, uncertainty and loss

A calculation can describe an assumption, but it cannot guarantee an outcome. Prices can move quickly, liquidity can change, and an order may execute at a different price from the one shown in an example. Costs, taxes and other conditions may also affect the final result. A neat spreadsheet is not a substitute for understanding those limits.

What position size means

Position size is the quantity represented by a hypothetical or actual position. In a simple arithmetic example, estimated value equals quantity multiplied by the example price. If a fictional share price is ₹200 and the example quantity is 3, the value is ₹600 before costs. This is a calculation, not a recommendation about how many shares anyone should own.

A simplified risk-distance example

Some educational calculators compare an example entry level with a hypothetical stop level. The distance between those two numbers can be used in a simplified formula. For instance, an example entry of ₹100 and a hypothetical exit level of ₹95 creates a ₹5 difference per unit. For 10 units, the arithmetic difference is ₹50 before costs and assuming the exit occurs at the specified level.

Why the example is limited

A stop instruction does not guarantee execution at its trigger level. Prices can gap past a level, orders can be partially filled, and real costs may apply. The example ignores those factors and must not be interpreted as a maximum guaranteed loss.

Why percentages can mislead

A percentage is only meaningful when its base is clear. One percent of ₹1,000 is ₹10; one percent of ₹100,000 is ₹1,000. A percentage does not determine whether a decision is appropriate. Personal finances, goals, time horizon and ability to tolerate losses differ, so a generic rule cannot assess an individual situation.

Common reasoning mistakes

  • Treating a calculator result as a prediction.
  • Assuming that a planned exit will always fill at the exact level.
  • Ignoring costs, gaps, liquidity and changing conditions.
  • Confusing a hypothetical practice result with actual performance.
  • Changing assumptions after seeing the result without recording why.

A more useful learning habit

When trying a scenario, write down the assumptions first, calculate the result, and then list what the formula leaves out. Compare scenarios to learn how the inputs affect the output—not to find a guaranteed winning setting. Keep a record of what you understood and what remains uncertain.

Key takeaway

Risk tools are aids for structured thinking. Their output is only as realistic as their assumptions, and no simplified calculation can remove uncertainty or guarantee a particular result.
