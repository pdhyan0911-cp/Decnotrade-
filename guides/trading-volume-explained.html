Trading volume explained: what the bars actually measure

Trading volume counts how much of an instrument changed hands during a chosen period. It can add context to a price chart, but it does not tell you on its own whether a price will rise or fall.

What does volume measure?

For a traded instrument, volume is commonly the number of units traded during a defined interval. Depending on the market and data provider, volume may be reported as shares, contracts or another unit. A chart's volume bar summarises activity for the interval shown on the horizontal axis.

How to read a volume bar

A taller bar generally represents more reported trading activity than a shorter bar over the same kind of interval and data source. Compare like with like: a five-minute bar should not be casually compared with a full-day bar. Session timing, holidays, instrument type and provider methodology can affect the numbers.

Hypothetical example

If one 15-minute interval reports 12,000 shares traded and the next reports 7,000, the first interval had 5,000 more shares of reported volume. This describes activity only; it does not explain who traded, why they traded, or what happens next.

Volume and liquidity are different

Volume describes completed trading over a period. Liquidity describes how easily participants may transact without causing a substantial price change. They are related, but a high-volume period can still include sharp price movement, and a low-volume instrument can have a wide bid-ask spread.

Does high volume mean a price will continue?

No. Volume is historical activity, not a forecast. The same increase in volume can occur in different market contexts and for different reasons. A volume bar cannot establish intent, confirm a future direction or guarantee that an order can be executed at a displayed price.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing volume bars from different timeframes as if they were equivalent.
  • Assuming high volume is automatically bullish or bearish.
  • Ignoring the instrument, exchange session and data source.
  • Treating generated or illustrative volume as live exchange information.

Questions to ask when viewing volume

  • What unit is the chart reporting?
  • What timeframe does each bar represent?
  • Is the data live, delayed, historical or simulated?
  • Am I describing activity, or making an unsupported prediction?

Key takeaway

Volume helps describe how much trading occurred during an interval. It is one piece of context, not a standalone signal or guarantee. Learn the chart's units and timeframe before drawing conclusions.
