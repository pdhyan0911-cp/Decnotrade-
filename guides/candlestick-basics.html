GUIDE 03 · CHART READING

Candlestick basics: reading four prices

A candlestick is a compact way to show how a price changed during a chosen time interval. It summarises four values, but it does not show every trade or explain why the movement occurred.

The four values

  • Open: the first recorded price for the interval under the chart's data rules.
  • High: the highest recorded price during the interval.
  • Low: the lowest recorded price during the interval.
  • Close: the final recorded price for the interval under the chart's data rules.

These are often shortened to OHLC. A chart's exact definitions can depend on the data provider, market session and selected timeframe.

Body and wicks

The candle body connects the open and close. If the close is above the open, many charts use one colour to show an upward interval. If the close is below the open, they often use another colour for a downward interval. Colours vary by chart settings, so learn the chart's legend instead of assuming a universal colour scheme.

The thin lines extending above or below the body are commonly called wicks or shadows. They show the high and low relative to the body. A long wick means the price reached farther from the open-close range during that interval; it does not, by itself, tell you what will happen next.

Timeframe changes the story

A one-minute candle summarises a different interval from a daily candle. Several short candles may fit inside one longer candle. A chart can therefore look different depending on the timeframe, the session boundaries and the values included in the data. Always check the selected interval before comparing candles.

Worked example

Imagine a candle with open ₹100, high ₹108, low ₹97 and close ₹105. The close is ₹5 above the open, while the interval's recorded range is ₹97 to ₹108. The body describes the open-to-close difference; the full high-low range includes the wicks. This example uses made-up values and says nothing about a real instrument.

What one candle cannot tell you

A candle does not establish the cause of a move, identify all participants, or guarantee a future direction. Similar-looking candles can appear in very different contexts. Volume, broader price history, news, liquidity and market conditions may provide additional context, but no single combination removes uncertainty.

Frequent beginner mistakes

  • Reading a candle without checking its timeframe.
  • Assuming a colour means a guaranteed future direction.
  • Treating an illustrative chart as live market information.
  • Ignoring the axis scale, session boundaries or data source.
  • Using one pattern as a certain prediction rather than a description of past prices.

Practice observing, not predicting

For each candle, try stating only what is visible: where it opened and closed, the high-low range, and how that range compares with the previous interval. Then separate those observations from any interpretation. This helps distinguish recorded information from a guess about the future.

Key takeaway

Candlesticks are a visual summary of past prices. They are useful for describing an interval, but they are not a promise, signal guarantee or substitute for a broader understanding of uncertainty.
