GUIDE 05 · CHART CONCEPTS

Support and resistance: understanding price areas

Support and resistance are chart-reading terms for areas where price has previously paused, reversed or struggled to move through. They are observations about past behaviour—not fixed barriers or promises about the future.

What is a support area?

Support is a term used for a price area where a decline has previously slowed or where price has rebounded. It is often discussed as an area rather than one exact number because trades occur across a range and charts depend on timeframe and data.

What is a resistance area?

Resistance describes an area where an advance has previously slowed or where price has turned lower. As with support, it is not a physical ceiling. Price can move through an area, stop short of it, or behave differently on another timeframe.

Hypothetical chart observation

Imagine a sample chart where price paused near ₹100 on several occasions and later struggled near ₹110. A learner might label ₹100 as a possible support area and ₹110 as a possible resistance area. Those labels summarise past observations; they do not establish that price must stay between them.

Why use zones instead of exact lines?

Different participants may use different chart periods, prices and definitions. A price may briefly move beyond a previous turning point and then return. Drawing a broad zone can communicate uncertainty more honestly than pretending a single precise number will always matter.

Can support become resistance?

Some chart explanations describe a former support area acting later as resistance, or the reverse. This is a way of describing a possible change in how a price area behaves. It is not a rule: the area may have no noticeable effect on a later visit.

Timeframe and context matter

An area visible on a daily chart may look different on a short intraday chart. The selected data, session, scale and instrument all affect what is visible. A chart pattern alone does not reveal every order, participant or reason behind a move.

Common mistakes

  • Drawing levels after the fact and assuming they were obvious beforehand.
  • Believing price must reverse at a marked area.
  • Ignoring the timeframe or changing the scale without noticing.
  • Treating one reaction as proof that a level will work again.
  • Confusing an illustrative chart with live market data.

A neutral practice exercise

On a historical or sample chart, mark two or three areas where price previously paused. Write down the timeframe and the evidence for each area. Then record later instances where price passed through it or did not react. The goal is to test how descriptive labels behave—not to create a guaranteed trading rule.

Key takeaway

Support and resistance are approximate ways to describe previous price behaviour. They can help organise chart observations, but they are subjective and cannot predict or guarantee future movement.
